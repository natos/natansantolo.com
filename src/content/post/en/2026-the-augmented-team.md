### Why I Wrote ["The Augmented Team"](/the-augmented-team/)

_What happens when you try to scale AI augmentation from one designer to six, across three timezones—and accidentally transform the entire organization_

Last year I published *The Augmented Designer*. It was about individual transformation—new tools, new archetypes, new ways of thinking about craft. I was proud of it. I still am.

But sitting here a year later, I have to admit something: I wrote about the wrong thing.

Not wrong exactly. Incomplete. The book described what happens when *one* designer augments themselves with AI. That's interesting. What I didn't predict—what nobody in the conversation was predicting—was what happens when you try to augment an entire *team*. And then what happens when that team, almost by accident, starts augmenting everyone around them.

That's the story I had to tell.

### The Context I Left Out

My team is six designers spread across Singapore, Berlin, and Bangalore. Fully remote. Supporting an engineering organization of over a hundred people. There's rarely an hour when all of us are awake at the same time.

Do the math: you cannot be the designer in the room when the room is in three countries and half of it is asleep.

That single constraint shaped everything. Most of what looks like clever strategy in *The Augmented Team* began as me trying to solve a scheduling problem—how do six people cover the design needs of a company that never stops, without burning out or becoming a queue everyone resents?

The answer wasn't "work harder" or "hire faster." It was five messy, overlapping phases that sometimes ran backwards.

### The Five Phases (That Weren't in the Plan)

**Phase 1: The Individual.** That was the first book. Me experimenting with AI tools, writing about it, figuring out what "augmented" even meant. 2025.

**Phase 2: The Self.** I started building personal infrastructure—a knowledge vault managed by an AI agent, a vision framework, structured thinking tools. This wasn't about *using* AI. It was about redesigning *how I think*, with AI as a partner.

**Phase 3: The Infrastructure.** Named agents with personalities and expertise. Design tokens shipped as an NPM package. Copilot skills that taught AI our brand rules. This is where the work stopped being about me and started becoming something other people could pick up.

**Phase 4: The Team.** Designers learning git. Designers shipping code. Designers writing agent instructions. Designers producing competitive analyses that changed product strategy. This was the hardest phase—and the one the first book barely imagined.

**Phase 5: The Organisation.** The part nobody predicted. The biggest impact wasn't on the design team. It was on everyone else. We shipped design tokens as an NPM package—and every engineer's output was suddenly on-brand by default. One designer spent two hours building a presentation system—and developers started using it for company presentations. We wrote one blog post about how we designed our product icons—and non-designers started borrowing the vocabulary of craft.

The arc wasn't tool adoption. It was **letting go**: we stopped trying to do all the design, and started building the things that let everyone do better design without us in the loop.

### The Conductor Became The Coach

The first book's most confident prediction was the Conductor archetype. You'd manage AI agents like a creative director managing a studio, and your job would be creative direction and quality control.

Partially true. We do have named agents. **Messi** orchestrates work. **Gabriela** manages our knowledge base. **Dieter** runs design critique. **Amos** handles behavioral psychology. **Borges** helps with writing. **Ada** ships code. They're genuinely useful. They're part of how we work now.

But the hardest part of my job in 2026 wasn't orchestrating agents. It was **coaching humans through the fear of the tools**.

Before anyone could work with AI, they had to learn git. Branches, commits, merge requests—the whole grammar of software collaboration, handed to people who'd spent their careers in Figma. One designer told me pushing code felt "like sending an email you can never unsend, except the email is the entire website."

I watched genuinely talented people whisper "I'm not a real developer" while, in the same week, shipping code engineers were running in production. I heard the deeper question underneath: *Am I still a designer, or am I becoming something else?*

The book said I'd be a Conductor. Reality asked for a Coach. Someone who builds confidence without dropping standards. Someone who celebrates a first merge request as loudly as a shipped product. Someone who makes it safe to break things and learn.

### The Signals We Didn't Recognize

The first signal was quiet. On March 3, 2026, I published `@affinidi/design-tokens`—a single NPM package with every brand guideline, UI token, and look-and-feel rule the company needed. One command: `npm install`.

From that moment, every developer had the full brand in their project. No Figma file to interpret. No PDF to read. No design request to file.

I built it to save myself from answering the same hex-code question at 11pm because the person asking was in a timezone where I was the only designer awake. Then I noticed the side effect: the baseline quality of demos, releases, and internal tools rose across the entire company—without a single designer touching the delivery pipeline.

The second signal was a company hackathon. I'd built a presentation system—Copilot skills that folded narrative structure, glassmorphism, and our design tokens into a pipeline. Write markdown, run the pipeline, get a cinematic deck. I dropped it into the shared repo and said "help yourselves."

By the second company presentation, developers were already using it—no training, no mandate, no request. Their slides looked like they'd come from a studio. The first time I watched someone else's deck built on my system, I felt two things at once: proud, and slightly redundant.

I've come to think that discomfort is the whole point.

The third signal was a blog post about how we designed our product logos. Five principles, the iteration, the reasoning. Then non-designers started *using the vocabulary*. "Does this violate the negative space rule?" An engineer said that, in a review, unprompted.

Three signals. Three flavors of the same instinct: **encode craft into infrastructure, distribute it through channels people already use, and let go**.

### What I Actually Learned

**The AI wasn't the hard part.** The terminal was. Git was. Trusting yourself to ship code was. Before anyone could dance with AI, they had to learn the steps—and that meant confronting imposter syndrome, redefining what "designer" means, and building confidence in unfamiliar territory.

**The highest-impact work wasn't the design itself.** It was making design thinking visible and accessible to people who'd never open Figma. The multiplier wasn't a clever AI trick. It was infrastructure, documentation, and removing ourselves from the critical path.

**Naming matters.** Our agents have names, personalities, and domains. Messi. Gabriela. Dieter. This wasn't cute branding—it created shared language. "Run it past Dieter" means something specific. "Ask Amos about the pricing page" produces a real deliverable.

**Designer skills transferred.** Writing good agent instructions turned out to be exactly like writing good design principles. Too vague and you get generic output. Too rigid and it becomes robotic. The sweet spot is specific constraints with flexibility for edge cases. Our designers were excellent at this—they'd spent years doing exactly that for components and patterns.

### The Future I'm Building

I don't want AI to replace designers. I want AI-augmented *teams* building systems that make everyone around them better at design. Where we encode craft into infrastructure. Where we coach, not control. Where we multiply impact by distributing capability.

But that future requires more than AI literacy. It requires designers who can think in systems, work in code, and teach machines while staying human. Who can build the thing that makes the thing.

### Your Turn

*The Augmented Designer* was about individual transformation. *The Augmented Team* is about collective transformation—and the messier, harder, more rewarding work of bringing people along with you.

If you're a design leader wondering how AI scales beyond you, this book is that story. Not the theory. What actually happened, including the parts that hurt and the parts I got wrong.

The hardest lessons aren't in the AI. They're in the coaching, the infrastructure, and learning when to let go.
